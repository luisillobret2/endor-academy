Open-Source Licenses, Obligations, and Conflicts

An open-source license is a legal contract that governs how a package can be used, modified, and redistributed. Every dependency you adopt — direct or transitive — brings its license obligations into your product. For proprietary software, the license terms matter as much as the vulnerability count.

The two families to understand first:

  • Permissive licenses (MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD) ask for little beyond attribution — generally safe to embed in commercial products.
  • Copyleft licenses (the GPL family) require that derivative works be distributed under the same license. Embedding GPL-licensed code in a proprietary product can obligate you to open-source it — or expose you to legal claims if you do not.
Category Examples Risk in Proprietary Products Key Obligation
Permissive MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD 2/3-Clause Low Retain copyright notices and license text
Weak copyleft LGPL, MPL 2.0, EPL Medium Share modifications to the covered component itself
Strong copyleft GPL-2.0, GPL-3.0, AGPL-3.0 High Release derivative works under the same license
Commercial / custom Vendor-specific terms Variable Honor the vendor contract — may require paid rights
No license detected Unlicensed code High All rights reserved by default — no permission to use

⚠️ Important for Partners: Legal Review Is Non-Negotiable

Endor Labs detects licenses and enforces your policy — it does not make legal decisions for you. Always recommend that customers involve legal counsel to define which licenses are approved, restricted, or banned for their products. Position Endor Labs as the enforcement and evidence layer for the policy legal teams define.

Endor Labs License Detection and Policies

Endor Labs identifies the license for every direct and transitive dependency in your projects, including dual-licensed packages where the effective license depends on how you use the package. This matters because the licenses that cause the most trouble are usually the ones nobody chose on purpose — a permissive library that quietly pulls in a GPL-licensed helper.

What License Policies Can Do

  • Block risky licenses — prevent GPL-family copyleft code from entering a proprietary product at all
  • Flag for review — surface weak-copyleft or custom licenses to security and legal stakeholders without blocking work
  • Restrict specific licenses — name individual licenses your organization will not accept, beyond the default categories
  • Scope by project — apply different rules to different applications, so an internal tool can carry a license your flagship product cannot
  • Enforce in pull requests — license policies run in PR scans, so a banned license fails the check before merge (covered in Module 07)

💡 Common Customer Scenario

A financial services company builds a proprietary trading platform. A developer adds a small open-source utility that turns out to be GPL-3.0 licensed — a transitive dependency of a package they actually chose. Endor Labs flags the license policy violation in the pull request, with the license and package clearly identified. The developer swaps in an MIT-licensed alternative in minutes, and legal never has to unwind a shipped product.

License Inventory, Audits, and SBOM Export

Detection and blocking handle new code. Audits require the full picture: a complete license inventory of everything your organization ships, with evidence that each item complies with policy. Endor Labs produces that inventory from the same dependency data that powers vulnerability findings — one scan, two compliance outputs.

  1. Detect — every scan records the license of every direct and transitive dependency across your projects
  2. Enforce — license policies block or flag violations at pull request time, keeping the tree clean going forward
  3. Review exceptions — approved uses are documented as exceptions, so the inventory explains why each restricted license is present
  4. Export — the license inventory ships inside your CycloneDX or SPDX SBOM exports, giving customers and auditors a standardized artifact
  5. Evidence — reports and exception records back up SOC 2, FedRAMP, and similar audit questions about open-source governance

License compliance is increasingly a procurement gate: enterprise buyers ask for a software bill of materials precisely so their own teams can review what is inside. A clean, current license inventory turns that question from a weeks-long fire drill into a routine export. Module 06 covers SBOM and VEX formats in detail.
